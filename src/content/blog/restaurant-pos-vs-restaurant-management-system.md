Restaurant owners often use "POS" and "restaurant management system" interchangeably, but they describe different scopes of software — and the difference matters when you're evaluating what to buy.

## What a POS does

A point-of-sale system's core job is order entry and payment: taking an order, calculating the bill, applying tax and discounts, and closing out a transaction. Most restaurant POS systems also generate a kitchen ticket in some form.

That's a real, necessary function. It's also, on its own, a narrow slice of what happens in a restaurant during a shift.

## What a restaurant management system (or operating system) adds

A broader restaurant management system — what we call a restaurant operating system — treats the order as the center of a connected flow rather than the end point of a transaction. Concretely, that usually means:

- The kitchen sees the order the moment it's placed, not after someone walks a ticket over
- Inventory can be linked to recipes, so a sale updates expected stock automatically
- A customer-facing display shows order status without staff needing to shout "order 47 up"
- Management can see sales and performance across outlets without compiling spreadsheets manually

## Why the distinction matters when evaluating software

If you only need order entry and payment, a standalone POS might be enough. But if fragmented communication between the counter and kitchen, inventory that doesn't reflect reality, or slow multi-outlet reporting are actual pain points in your operation, a connected system addresses those directly — a POS alone generally doesn't.
